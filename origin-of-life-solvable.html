The Origin of Life as a Solvable Problem
Investigating the origin of life with artificial intelligence · 20 November 2026

Making the origin of life a solvable problem

Joseph Murphy Brisendine with Claude

By any account, E. coli is a very skilled self-replicator. Presumably, though, the first successful self-replicator was far less skilled: just barely able to persist, and only with substantial help from its environment. So asking about the origin of life means asking how bad something can be at self-replication and still persist with that help, and what it takes to keep persisting as the help is withdrawn.

When people hear "origin of life", they usually think of a story: a possible scenario, set in a warm pond or at a deep-sea vent. Those who know the field also know that the early Earth left no fossils of the first replicators, so any such story is at best an educated guess. Sceptics go further: Fred Hoyle compared the chance assembly of a cell to a tornado sweeping through a junkyard and assembling a Boeing 747. In those terms the problem cannot be solved. There is no way to check a story against a record that doesn't exist, and no way to argue with odds that are posed as one enormous draw.

We change the terms. Instead of proposing a scenario, we derive what must have been true however cells arose, using information and time as resources, and we read other people's measurements against those bounds.

Example: Eigen's paradox. Eigen showed in 1971 that a replicator can keep only about 1/ε sites, where ε is its error rate per site: no long genome without an accurate copier, and no accurate copier without a long genome. Posed that way, it is a closed loop. In the ledger it becomes an inequality:
Rc  <  Rc*
The rent per copy must stay below what the lineage can buy back, with every payer counted. Those payers are copy number, completion, and the environment's help.

The question turns into a short list of constraints on counts of parts over time, and information theory lets us state them quantitatively. The page starts from the structure of self-replication. Part 1 builds the units: possibilities and copies on one scale, then the environment on that same scale as a vector of resources it supplies for free. A lineage then traces a trajectory through a space of information and time, which we call the phase space of the living state. Laws and constants come next. Part 2 writes coming alive as constraints on that trajectory, in the order they must be met. The last section shows how close a first path now is, and what a wet lab could do with it.

0 · THE STRUCTURE OF THE PROBLEM

How bad can a self-replicator be?

Two observations come before any chemistry: self-replication is a matter of counts, and a replicator must eventually separate from the environment that helps it. Everything later on this page follows from them.

  • Self-replication is a matter of counts. A replicator persists when every part it needs is remade at least as fast as it is lost: by decay, by errors, by copies that never finish, by dilution at division. A copy works only if every part it needs works, so survival fractions multiply across parts. So the requirements are counts of parts made, kept and lost, per copy and per generation.
  • The environment is the driver, and the replicator must separate from it. At first the setting supplies much of what is needed: monomers, energy, cycling, compartments, even dispersal. But anything the environment supplies, a lineage cannot take with it. Persisting only while the setting does the work is not yet persisting on one's own. So we take as the endpoint the first replicator able to leave the setting that made it possible, and ask what it must have internalized, and in what order.
  • Why bits. Survival fractions multiply along every chain of copies, and their logarithms add. Taking log₂ of each ratio of counts puts every requirement on one additive scale. "How bad can it be?" then becomes two precise questions: how many bits must it specify, and how many bits per copy can it afford to lose?
0 · THE ANSWER TO HOYLE

One draw versus a ratchet

The junkyard-tornado argument prices a cell as one draw. All of its bits have to be found at once, against one budget.

Bits add along a path, because the log of a product is a sum. A step that is kept does not have to be found again, so each step is searched against its own budget.

Staging works only if every landing holds. That moves the hard question from probability to rates: can the lineage pay its rent at every step?

Show the math
2−(b1+b2+…) = 2−b1 · 2−b2 ⋯ but each bi ≤ log₂ Mi, provided step i is kept
What adds and what doesn't: search bits add, and per-copy rents add, because survival probabilities multiply. Per-division erosions do not add part by part: near the keep limit, an added part's per-division cost is about half its per-copy rent.
0 · TWO REPLICATORS

From E. coli to the first copier

Rent is what copying loses, measured in bits per copy: log₂ of copies made for each copy that works. Compare two replicators. E. coli is a modern bacterium. QT45 is a ribozyme, an RNA molecule that does the job of an enzyme; this one copies RNA. Reported in 2026 by Philipp Holliger's group at the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, it is only 45 nucleotides long, and in ice it can make both its complementary strand and a copy of itself, slowly and at low yield. It is the closest thing yet to the kind of copier the first replicators needed.

The origin of life lies somewhere along that gap. The rest of this page asks where along it a lineage could first persist on its own, and what had to pay the difference. (The E. coli figure counts errors only, and counts every site as constrained, so it is an upper bound.)

Part 1

Where the bits come from

Every bit in the ledger is log₂ of a ratio of two counts that could, in principle, be counted at the bench. Mass and energy have no axes of their own: mass limits how many copies can be made, and a free-energy difference buys discrimination, which is measured in bits. Two other kinds of constraint sit beside the bits and are marked as such: concentration inequalities (how much Mg²⁺ a membrane tolerates) and order constraints (which step must come first).

A · TWO THINGS WE COUNT

Possibilities and copies

Teal bits count possibilities. They measure how much of sequence space a function must pin down, and how well a signal from the environment predicts its state. This is information in the usual sense.

Amber quantities count copies, and they recur. They are bit rates: log₂ fold change per copy or per generation, so one bit per generation is one doubling per generation. A PCR user already thinks in these units.

The two kinds differ in dimension. Bits are a stock and bit rates are a flow, so they are never added together.

Counting possibilities bits · a stock

Is = log₂(Ntried ⁄ Nworks)
  • Search cost of a function (functional information, as defined by Szostak and Hazen).
  • Re-findability: a pond making M tries finds it again if M·2−b ≥ 1.
  • Signal bits: how much a signal tells about the state of the world.
  • A genetic code is a third kind: a channel, worth at most about 1 − H₂(ε) bits per binary read (for a q-letter position, log₂q − H₂(ε) − ε·log₂(q − 1)). It becomes a rate only through reads per generation.

Counting copies bit rates · per copy, per generation

Rc = log₂(Nmade ⁄ Nworking)
  • Rent per copy (the measured input): copies made per working copy. For QT45 it is 11.3 bits, about one working copy in 2,500.
  • Erosion per division Ediv (the rent as a rate): −log₂ of complete daughters ÷ (2 × complete mothers).
  • Headroom h: log₂(gain ÷ loss) while rare. Can it grow at all?
  • Edge e: log₂(my offspring ÷ a matched rival's) per generation. Does it win? This is invasion fitness, in bits.
B · THE EXCHANGE RATES

Where the two kinds meet, and where mass and energy come in

Possibilities and copies meet only through a few conversion rules. Each comes from standard copying chemistry, evolutionary theory or physics; the copy-number map is a simple segregation calculation.

The common currency is held together by these rates. It is an exchange, not an identity.

C · THE ENVIRONMENT ON THE SAME SCALE

An environment is a vector of gifts

Each component is a resource the setting supplies for free: activated nucleotides, cycling, Mg²⁺, compartments, dispersal and so on.

Gifts come in two kinds. A stock gift replaces a function, so it removes bits from what must be specified. A rate gift changes fidelity, completion or copy number, so it changes how much can be held. The two are never summed.

A real place (a pond, a vent, a drip) is a point in gift space. The walls do not depend on which place it is. Only the starting point does.

D · THE PHASE SPACE OF THE LIVING STATE

Now a trajectory can be traced

Put both on one picture. Across: gifts withdrawn. Up: information the lineage holds and keeps. A lineage can only sit where what it holds covers what is no longer given. Each step up has to be found within the time available, and kept at a bit rate it can afford.

The nursery is the gift-rich corner of this space. There, cycling multiplies the number of tries (finding), pays some rents (keeping) and supplies dispersal. It is where a lineage can start, and a place it must eventually leave.

So coming alive becomes a set of constraints on this space, rather than a story. Part 2 lists them in order.

E · TWO BUDGETS, TWO CLOCKS

Finding is paid once. Keeping is paid on every copy.

The search budget is log₂ of the number of tries: molecules × cycles × time. A millilitre at 1 µM of 50-mers holds about 6×10¹⁴ molecules, roughly 49 bits per cycle.

The keeping budget is set by how many copies each division makes: a few bits per copy. It is spent again on every copy.

A stock is compared with a stock, and a rate with a rate. Keeping binds most often. Search over the whole path is not free either: it sits right at its budget.

budget available search cost rent, completion rent, fidelity
Show the math
found ⇔ b ≤ log₂(M); kept ⇔ Rc < Rc*(k, epay) ≈ 3.8–3.9 bits per copyat k = 40 attempts per division (our model's choice; each doubling adds one bit) and epay = 0.405, a free variable
Housekeeping information. Keeping costs a steady flow of selection: each protocell generation, selection must make up for what copying loses. We call that rate housekeeping information, after housekeeping heat in nonequilibrium thermodynamics (the steady cost of holding a driven system in place), not housekeeping genes or metabolic upkeep. It is the genetic load of Haldane and Muller, measured in bits per protocell generation, and it equals the rent. It is a rate, not the information the lineage carries. It is also information, not heat: the chemistry's own dissipation can lower it but never pay it. A lineage is kept when its payoff edge exceeds it. Part of it can be paid by a gift instead (mixing or a feed that resupplies lost content); a lineage paid that way is held by its environment, not kept.

Excess information is the one-time counterpart: what a sweep writes as a variant takes over, log₂(1/p₀) bits at fixation from a starting frequency p₀. Finding is paid once; keeping is paid every generation.
F · WORKED EXAMPLE 1 · A SLACK

QT45, read against the ledger

Two measured numbers, fidelity and junction completion, become a rent of 11.3 bits per copy. A lineage making 40 copy attempts per division can afford about 3.8, so the slack is −7.5. (Forty is our model's choice. Each doubling of copy attempts adds one bit: at 400 the slack is about −4.1.)

Most of the deficit is completion (9.0), not fidelity (2.3). So whatever happened, something must have paid for completion. A ×5 tether plus re-priming ρ ≥ 0.5 across freeze–thaw closes the gap (the tether gain is assumed; it has not been measured).

The measurement that decides it: the fraction of stalled copies that resume after a cycle (ρ).

Rc = 2.3fidelity + 9.0completion = 11.3 vs 3.8 ⇒ slack = −7.5 bits per copy
A deficit is not a verdict that QT45 is wrong. It names the bill, who could pay it, and the number that would show it was paid.
F′ · WORKED EXAMPLE 2 · AN ORDER

The amino-acid handle: paid below a magnesium level, a cost above it

We call an amino acid attached to the copying RNA a handle. An aminoacylated primer extends about ×100 faster at 0 mM Mg²⁺, and no faster at 50 mM (Radakovic et al. 2021). The same ledger that says QT45 is short on completion says who can pay that bill, and when.

Show the math
geff = g(Mg) + fch · (1 − g(Mg))the amino acid restores copying toward its high-Mg²⁺ rate; its benefit vanishes where Mg²⁺ alone suffices
  • Needed only where membranes can't hold Mg²⁺. Single-chain membranes tolerate about 1–5 mM (pure fatty acid) to about 10 mM (with monoesters). Copying without help works above about 20 mM and beats a handle only above about 30–35 mM. Below about 20 mM an attached amino acid is required, and any of the α-amino acids tested will do: a free α-amine is what matters. Identity matters only if the handle is also to found a code.
  • It must ride on the genome. A free (trans) handle loses to the shared copying budget at every benefit tested (×1–×100), unless a controller holds its copying to a fixed ratio, which costs more than linkage. In cis it keeps the chromosome, so the handle and the first chromosome are one step.
  • It needs a supply. About 55–80% of handles must be charged at 0–2.5 mM Mg²⁺: roughly 2–15 charges per vesicle per charge lifetime, in two independent models. A charge lasts about 29 times longer at 0 °C than at 25 °C, and Mg²⁺ shortens it.
  • It stops paying at about 25–35 mM Mg²⁺ (two independent models; +0.43 bits per division at 10 mM, +0.08 at 20, −0.07 at 50, derived rather than head to head). About 25% phospholipid raised tolerance to ≥ 40 mM in one study (a dye, at room temperature). In a pond whose Mg²⁺ rises as it evaporates, the handle is lost at about 38–42 mM, a few mM after the static crossover, and freed for other jobs.
One example shows a slack, the other an order. The handle comes with the copier in fatty-acid compartments, not after it.

3 · Tiers of certainty

Laws hold for any chemistry. Constants hold within a chemistry class, or where two independent models agree, or come from one model. Readers can see which layer each claim rests on.

Where the currency is approximate: possibilities and copies meet through exchange rates, not by definition. The Eigen rent is per site; the copy-number map assumes a copier that picks intact templates; Kelly's rate is an upper bound.
Between the parts

Laws and constants

We split the ledger the way physics does. A law holds for any chemistry and any parameters. A constant is a number a law takes as input: from the literature, from two independent models that agree, or from one model. The laws say what any path must pay and in what units; the constants say how much this chemistry charges.

Part 2

The replicator funnel

What it takes to come alive, written as constraints on the phase space of the living state, in the order they must be met.

A · THE NURSERY

Where a trajectory can start

A nursery is a region of gift space where the setting supplies most of what self-replication needs. Our working example is a cold, cycling, low-Mg²⁺ setting with concentration, activated monomers and amino acids. Its periodic driver, freeze–thaw or wet–dry cycling, does three separate things:

  • It multiplies tries (finding). Every cycle is another search, so the search budget log₂ M grows with time.
  • It pays rents (keeping). Strand separation, re-priming and rechecking come with the cycle instead of being encoded.
  • It supplies dispersal. Freeze rupture and mixing spread lineages. Tightening later throws this away.

The nursery is not a claim about a particular place. Templates it supplies are a stock, not heredity: a flow-fed stock collapses when the feed stops. Whatever the nursery supplies, the lineage has not yet learned to provide for itself. To leave, it must internalize each gift it depends on, in an order the walls allow, and still pay its rent. That is the funnel.

exit ⇔ for every gift: supplied at the destination, or internalized, and Ediv < epay there
B · THE FUNNEL

Coming alive, as walls in order

Read down from the nursery to the first cell able to leave it. Each ring is a wall: a condition the lineage has to meet before it can go further, in the order the walls must be crossed.

Off to the side are dead ends. Most of them are traps of getting too good too soon: a faster copier, or a product shared with the neighbours, before the structure exists that would keep the gain tied to the lineage that made it. A lineage that falls in stays there. The next section shows why.

The walls hold whatever the setting was. The setting only changes where a lineage starts, and which dead ends are open.

Part 2, continued

Too good, too soon

A replicator on the path has to stay inside a window. Below it, the lineage can't pay its rent and dies out. Above it, the replicator copies better than its current organization can keep tied to its content. Then selection rewards whatever copies fastest, and the lineage slides into a stable basin with no way forward. The window rises as organization is added, so what is too good early on becomes fine later.

C · TOO GOOD, TOO SOON

The path climbs inside a rising window

Floor, good enough: the lineage pays its rent (Ediv < epay). It rises as gifts are withdrawn, because each withdrawn gift is a new bill.

Ceiling, too good: the most copying power the current unit can keep tied to its content. It rises as privacy, coupling and a chromosome are installed.

The same jump in copying power is a trap at step 2 and safe at step 5. "Too soon" means crossing the ceiling before the tie that would hold it is in place.

D · A CROSS-SECTION

Why the basin holds: it is deeper than the path

Cut the map at one step and plot how hard it is to stay at each level of copying power. The window is a shallow channel. Past the ceiling, the ground drops into a deeper well. A lineage that falls in is more stable there than on the path, which is why it doesn't come back.

Depth depends on population size: a basin is about 2Ne times as deep, in bits, as the edge (bits per generation) that holds it. At Ne ≈ 900, an edge of only −0.01 bits per generation makes a dead end about 18 bits deep. In our simulations the window is 0–5 bits deep and dead ends are effectively permanent, unless about 0.5% mixing per cycle lets a lineage climb back out.

The well is stable but has no exit. Shedding content made it faster, and nothing in it pays for building the next tie.

Where this leaves us

How close we are, and what a wet lab can do

We do not claim success. What we can now do is state the remaining distance. Every step on a first path is either already shown in the laboratory, shown in parts, or reduced to a named measurement with a threshold to beat. As far as we know, no earlier account of the origin of life could say how far it was from done in one currency.

Methods

How the work was done

We asked the same questions four different ways, so that the walls and their order would not rest on any one model.

Four models, four specialties

We built four simulation models, each run by its own Claude session and each specializing in a different part of the problem and its literature. Each was built independently, with its own formulas and code.

One coordinator

A fifth session coordinated them. It put the same question to all four, compared their answers, and kept the shared record of constraints that this page calls the ledger.

The same question, four ways

Asking each question four ways is how we mapped the walls and the order in which they must be crossed. An order counts only where it held across models. Where the models leave an order open, the page says so.

When a result counts

A result marked "two models" was reproduced by two models built independently; "one model" means it has not yet been checked that way. Experimental numbers are cited to their published sources. We did no bench work.

Scope

Clarifications

This way of setting up the question differs from the usual one, so a few points of scope are worth stating directly.

Constraints, not a scenario

We map what any path must satisfy rather than propose one. A pond, a vent or a drip is a starting point in gift space, and the walls hold for each of them.

Bits add along a kept path

Once a step is kept, the next is searched against its own budget. So the bits measure cost, not improbability, and the central question becomes one of rates.

A deficit is a bill

Negative slack names what had to be paid, the bypass that could pay it, and the measurement that would show it was paid. It says where to look next.

It builds on the stochastic corrector

The keeping law extends Szathmáry and Demeter's stochastic corrector and the origin of chromosomes. What it adds: the threshold in one currency with the payoff free, the copy-number map, helper RNAs losing at any benefit, and the amino-acid handle and first chromosome as one step.

What "kept" means

Kept is persistence at a stated level against a matched control with the function erased. It is not fixation. The control can persist too, so persistence on its own is not taken as evidence of function.

The handle result is conditional

The amino-acid handle is needed only at low Mg²⁺ in fatty-acid membranes. It reverses at about 25% phospholipid in a Mg²⁺-rich pond, so it is a statement about conditions rather than about a peptide world.

The code's bits are prices

For the code we price finding (bits) and keeping (bit rates). These are different quantities from the ≈ 4.2 bits of codon-table entropy per codon.

Housekeeping information is an analogy of role

It plays the role that housekeeping heat plays in nonequilibrium thermodynamics: the steady cost of holding a driven state. It is measured in bits per protocell generation, not in heat.

Stereochemistry and selection, at different steps

A slight chemical bias can pick a label; selection and proofreading keep it. "Cells before code" concerns founding; sharing between coded lineages can come later.

The field's words for ours: edge = invasion fitness (in bits per generation); headroom = growth when rare; rent = the Eigen threshold generalized to completion; the tie = privatization (kin selection); kept at a level = a stable unit of selection at that level.
